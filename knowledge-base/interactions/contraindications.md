# Contraindications by Population

## Pregnancy

### Herbs to Avoid

| Herb | Reason |
|------|--------|
| Ashwagandha | Uterine stimulant; may cause premature contractions; abortifacient in high doses |
| Licorice (high dose) | Glycyrrhizin increases maternal BP, causes edema, linked to preterm birth; mineralocorticoid effects |
| Punarnava | Uterine stimulant; risk of abortion in early pregnancy |
| Arjuna | Insufficient safety data; potential cardiac effects on fetus |
| Guggulu | Uterine stimulant; may interfere with fetal development; abortifacient properties |
| Kutki | Uterine stimulant; insufficient safety data in pregnancy |
| Triphala (high dose) | Aperient/laxative effect may stimulate uterine contractions; low culinary doses generally acceptable |
| Aloe vera (internal) | Uterine stimulant; purgative action; risk of pelvic congestion |
| Castor oil | Strong purgative; uterine stimulant; historically used to induce labor |
| Dong quai | Uterine stimulant; anticoagulant; increased bleeding risk |
| Black cohosh | Uterine stimulant; estrogenic effects; linked to preterm labor |
| Blue cohosh | Uterine stimulant; toxic to infant; linked to neonatal heart failure |
| Pennyroyal | Potent abortifacient; hepatotoxic; potentially lethal in pregnancy |
| Ephedra (ma huang) | Vasoconstriction; increased maternal BP and fetal heart rate; risk of fetal hypoxia |
| Sage (high-dose medicinal) | Thujone content is uterotonic; emmenagogue |
| Rosemary (high-dose medicinal) | Uterine stimulant in concentrated forms |
| Feverfew | Uterine stimulant; risk of premature labor |
| Goldenseal | Uterine stimulant; berberine crosses placenta; potential fetal toxicity |

### Exercises and Practices to Avoid

- Hot yoga / saunas / steam baths -- hyperthermia risk in first trimester; linked to neural tube defects
- High-impact contact sports -- risk of abdominal trauma
- Supine exercises after first trimester -- compression of inferior vena cava; reduced uterine blood flow
- Deep twists and inversions (yoga) -- compression of abdomen; risk to placental attachment
- Scuba diving -- fetal decompression sickness risk
- Heavy weightlifting (maximal loads) -- increased intra-abdominal pressure; risk of preterm labor
- High-altitude exercise -- reduced fetal oxygenation above 2,500m after 20 weeks

## Breastfeeding

### Herbs That Reduce Milk Supply (Avoid)

| Herb | Reason |
|------|--------|
| Sage (high dose) | Anticholinergic; reduces prolactin; traditionally used to dry up milk |
| Peppermint (high dose) | May reduce milk supply in large quantities |
| Parsley (high dose) | Apiol content may reduce lactation |
| Oregano (high dose) | May reduce milk supply in concentrated forms |
| Thyme (high dose) | May reduce milk supply in medicinal quantities |
| Black walnut | Traditional galactagogue inhibitor |
| Yarrow | May reduce milk supply |

### Herbs That Increase Milk Supply (Use with Caution)

| Herb | Reason |
|------|--------|
| Fenugreek | Galactagogue; may cause GI upset in infant; maple-syrup odor in urine |
| Blessed thistle | Galactagogue; generally safe but monitor infant for GI effects |
| Shatavari | Ayurvedic galactagogue; traditionally used postpartum; generally safe |
| Fennel | Galactagogue; may pass into milk with mild estrogenic effect on infant |

### Herbs to Avoid While Breastfeeding

| Herb | Reason |
|------|--------|
| Sage, peppermint, parsley (high dose) | Reduce milk supply |
| Ashwagandha | Insufficient safety data; may affect infant through breast milk |
| Guggulu | May affect infant via lipid changes in milk |
| Ginseng | May cause irritability in nursing infant |
| Ephedra | Passes into breast milk; causes irritability, insomnia, poor feeding in infant |
| Kava | Passes into breast milk; potential hepatotoxicity for infant |
| Goldenseal | Berberine passes into milk; may affect infant liver function |

## Liver Disease

### Herbs to Avoid or Use with Extreme Caution

| Herb | Reason |
|------|--------|
| Kava | Hepatotoxic; linked to fulminant liver failure; contraindicated in any liver impairment |
| Black cohosh | Hepatotoxicity reports; contraindicated with pre-existing liver disease |
| Chaparral | Potent hepatotoxin; causes hepatocellular necrosis |
| Comfrey (internal) | Contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids; causes hepatic veno-occlusive disease |
| Germander | Hepatotoxic; causes hepatocellular damage |
| Pennyroyal | Hepatotoxic; can cause acute liver failure |
| Skullcap | Hepatotoxicity reports; some products adulterated with germander |
| Valerian (high dose) | Metabolized by liver; dose reduction required in hepatic impairment |
| St. John's Wort | Induces CYP450 enzymes; may alter metabolism of hepatoprotective drugs |
| Licorice (long-term, high dose) | Can cause hepatotoxicity and pseudoaldosteronism; monitor liver enzymes |

### General Dose Reduction Guidelines

- Reduce all herb doses by 50% in moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh B)
- Reduce by 75% in severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh C)
- Avoid herbs requiring hepatic metabolism (CYP450 substrates) in severe liver disease
- Monitor liver function tests (AST, ALT, bilirubin, INR) every 4-6 weeks when using herbal therapies
- Prefer herbs with renal or minimal hepatic elimination

## Kidney Disease

### Herbs to Avoid or Use with Caution

| Herb | Reason |
|------|--------|
| Licorice | Causes sodium and water retention; hypokalemia; worsens hypertension and kidney function |
| Aristolochia | Nephrotoxic; causes aristolochic acid nephropathy (AAN); linked to urothelial cancer |
| Star fruit (carambola) | Contains neurotoxin; cannot be cleared in CKD; causes seizures, death in dialysis patients |
| Juniper (high dose) | Irritates kidneys; diuretic effect may worsen dehydration and renal function |
| Coltsfoot | Contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids; nephrotoxic and hepatotoxic |
| Comfrey (internal) | Pyrrolizidine alkaloids cause renal and hepatic damage |
| Dandelion leaf | Potent diuretic; may cause electrolyte imbalance in CKD |
| Nettle leaf | Diuretic effect; may alter potassium balance |
| Parsley (high dose) | Diuretic; oxalate content risks kidney stone formation |
| Rhubarb root | Contains oxalates; risk of kidney stone formation; anthraquinones may damage renal tubules |
| Punarnava | Diuretic; may worsen electrolyte imbalance in advanced CKD |
| Vitamin C (high-dose supplements) | Metabolized to oxalate; risk of oxalate nephropathy and stones |

### General Guidelines

- Monitor creatinine, BUN, eGFR, electrolytes (especially potassium) every 4-6 weeks
- Avoid diuretic herbs in CKD stage 3b-5
- Reduce all herb doses by 50% in moderate CKD (eGFR 30-59)
- Avoid herbal supplements entirely in severe CKD (eGFR <30) unless directed by nephrologist
- Screen all herbal products for hidden potassium, phosphorus, or heavy metal content

## Bleeding Disorders / Anticoagulant Therapy

### Herbs with Anticoagulant or Antiplatelet Effects (Avoid or Monitor Closely)

| Herb | Mechanism | Risk Level |
|------|-----------|------------|
| Turmeric / Curcumin | Inhibits platelet aggregation; inhibits thromboxane B2; may potentiate warfarin | Moderate -- dietary amounts safe; avoid high-dose supplements |
| Ginger | Inhibits COX-1 and thromboxane synthesis; antiplatelet effect | Moderate -- avoid supplements; culinary amounts generally safe |
| Garlic | Ajoene and other sulfur compounds inhibit platelet aggregation | Moderate -- avoid aged garlic extract supplements; 1-2 cloves dietary generally safe |
| Ginkgo biloba | Ginkgolides inhibit PAF (platelet-activating factor) | High -- significantly increases bleeding risk; stop 2 weeks before surgery |
| Ginseng (Panax) | Antiplatelet; may decrease warfarin INR unpredictably | High -- avoid with warfarin; monitor closely with other anticoagulants |
| Dong quai | Coumarin content; anticoagulant effect | High -- avoid entirely with anticoagulant therapy |
| Feverfew | Inhibits platelet aggregation via PAF pathway | Moderate -- use with caution |
| Vitamin E (high dose) | Inhibits platelet aggregation; antagonizes vitamin K | Moderate -- avoid >400 IU/day with anticoagulants |
| Fish oil / Omega-3 (high dose) | Reduces thromboxane A2; antiplatelet effect | Moderate -- avoid >3g/day EPA+DHA with anticoagulants |
| Bilberry | Antiplatelet flavonoids (anthocyanins) | Low-moderate -- caution at supplement doses |
| Cayenne (capsaicin) | Antiplatelet effect at high doses | Low -- culinary amounts safe |
| Willow bark | Contains salicin (salicylate precursor); antiplatelet | Moderate -- avoid with warfarin and other anticoagulants |
| Reishi mushroom | Antiplatelet; inhibits aggregation | Low-moderate -- caution at supplement doses |
| Dan Shen (Salvia miltiorrhiza) | Potent anticoagulant; increases INR | High -- avoid with all anticoagulants |

### General Guidelines

- Stop all herbs with anticoagulant effects at least 14 days before any surgical or dental procedure
- Monitor INR weekly when starting or stopping any herb while on warfarin
- Advise patients to carry a list of all supplements and inform all providers
- Avoid combinations of multiple herbs with antiplatelet/anticoagulant effects

## Autoimmune Conditions

### Herbs to Avoid During Flares

| Herb | Reason |
|------|--------|
| Echinacea | Potent immune stimulant; may exacerbate autoimmune activity during flares (SLE, RA, MS) |
| Astragalus | Stimulates immune cell activity; may worsen autoimmune flares; theoretically contraindicated in MS and SLE |
| Ginseng (Panax) | Immune stimulant; may increase autoimmune activity |
| Cat's claw | Immune stimulant; may exacerbate RA and SLE |
| Alfalfa sprouts | Contain L-canavanine; linked to lupus flares; stimulates immune system |
| Mistletoe | Immune stimulant; may worsen autoimmune conditions |

### Herbs Generally Safe in Autoimmune Conditions

| Herb | Reason |
|------|--------|
| Turmeric / Curcumin | Immune-modulating (not stimulating); anti-inflammatory; may actually benefit RA and IBD |
| Ashwagandha | Adaptogenic immune modulation; may help regulate overactive immune response; generally safe |
| Boswellia (Indian frankincense) | Anti-inflammatory via 5-LOX inhibition; beneficial in RA, Crohn's, ulcerative colitis |
| Guduchi (Giloy) | Immunomodulator (balances rather than stimulates); traditional Ayurvedic rasayana |
| Triphala | Gentle immune modulation; antioxidant; generally safe |
| Ginger | Anti-inflammatory; no significant immune-stimulating activity |

### General Guidelines

- Immune-stimulating herbs are relatively contraindicated during active disease flares
- Immune-modulating herbs (turmeric, boswellia, ashwagandha) may be beneficial and are generally safe
- Reintroduce immune-stimulating herbs cautiously during remission only
- Monitor disease activity markers (ESR, CRP, ANA, specific autoantibodies) when using any herbal therapy
- Always coordinate with the patient's rheumatologist or treating specialist

## Children

### Age Restrictions and Safety Guidelines

| Age Group | Guidelines |
|-----------|-----------|
| Infants (0-12 months) | Avoid all herbal supplements unless prescribed by pediatrician; breast milk provides immune support |
| Toddlers (1-3 years) | Only gentle herbs: chamomile tea (for colic), fennel (for digestion); avoid all potent herbs; dose = 1/4 to 1/6 adult dose by weight |
| Young children (4-6 years) | May use mild herbs: chamomile, elderberry (for colds), ginger (for nausea); avoid ashwagandha, ginseng, licorice, ginkgo; dose = 1/4 to 1/3 adult dose |
| Older children (7-12 years) | Broader range with caution: turmeric, echinacea (short-term for colds), triphala (low dose); avoid ginkgo, ginseng, kava, St. John's Wort; dose = 1/2 adult dose |
| Adolescents (13-17 years) | May use most herbs at reduced doses; avoid St. John's Wort (SSRI interaction risk); avoid kava; dose = 3/4 adult dose |

### Herbs Strictly Contraindicated in Children

| Herb | Reason |
|------|--------|
| Kava | Hepatotoxicity risk; no safe pediatric dose established |
| St. John's Wort | CYP interactions; serotonin syndrome risk with SSRIs |
| Ephedra (ma huang) | Cardiovascular stimulation; deaths reported in adolescents; banned in many countries |
| Pennyroyal | Hepatotoxic; potentially lethal even in small doses |
| Wormwood | Neurotoxic (thujone); hallucinations; seizures |
| Yohimbe | Cardiovascular toxicity; severe hypertension |
| Comfrey (internal) | Hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids |
| Coltsfoot (internal) | Hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids |
| Chaparral | Hepatotoxic |

### General Pediatric Guidelines

- Always use the lowest effective dose
- Prefer gentle administration forms (teas, glycerites) over concentrated tinctures
- Avoid alcohol-based tinctures in children under 5
- Start with single herbs rather than complex formulations
- Monitor for allergic reactions (especially with echinacea, chamomile -- asteraceae family)
- Discontinue immediately if adverse effects occur
- Consult pediatrician before introducing any herbal supplement

## Elderly (65+)

### Dose Modification Guidelines

| Parameter | Recommendation |
|-----------|----------------|
| Starting dose | 50% of standard adult dose |
| Titration | Increase gradually every 2-4 weeks; monitor response |
| Maximum dose | Rarely exceed 75% of standard adult dose |
| Polypharmacy consideration | Screen all herbs for CYP450 interactions with current medications |
| Renal/hepatic impairment | Apply additional dose reductions per those sections above |

### Herbs Requiring Extra Caution in Elderly

| Herb | Reason |
|------|--------|
| Ginkgo biloba | Increased bleeding risk (higher baseline fall risk, often on aspirin/anticoagulants) |
| Ginseng | May cause insomnia, agitation, hypertension; interacts with warfarin and diabetes medications |
| Garlic supplements | Antiplatelet effect; many elderly are on aspirin or anticoagulants |
| Licorice | Raises BP, causes hypokalemia, fluid retention; dangerous with concurrent hypertension/diuretics |
| St. John's Wort | Massive CYP interaction profile; dangerous with polypharmacy; reduces efficacy of many critical drugs |
| Kava | Hepatotoxicity risk increases with age; CNS effects amplified by age-related metabolism changes |
| Ephedra / Ma huang | Cardiovascular stimulation especially dangerous in elderly with hypertension, CAD, arrhythmias |
| Evening primrose oil | May increase seizure risk; interacts with anticoagulants |
| Valerian (high dose) | Prolonged sedation, confusion, increased fall risk; start at very low doses |
| Senna / Cascara (stimulant laxatives) | Electrolyte imbalance; may worsen constipation with chronic use (melanosis coli) |

### General Guidelines

- Prioritize safety over efficacy; start low, go slow
- Comprehensive medication reconciliation before adding any herb
- Monitor renal function (eGFR) and liver function (AST/ALT) at baseline and periodically
- Watch for orthostatic hypotension (fall risk) with any new herb
- Simplify regimens; avoid complex multi-herb formulations when possible
- Educate caregivers on signs of adverse effects
- Consider drug-herb interactions as a major cause of preventable adverse events in this population

## Post-Surgery

### Herbs to Discontinue 2 Weeks Before Surgery

| Herb | Reason | Minimum Stop Time |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Ginkgo biloba | Antiplatelet (PAF inhibition); bleeding risk | 14 days |
| Garlic (supplements) | Antiplatelet (ajoene); bleeding risk | 7-14 days |
| Ginseng (Panax) | Antiplatelet; affects blood sugar; bleeding risk | 7 days |
| Ginger (supplements) | Antiplatelet; inhibits thromboxane | 7 days |
| Turmeric / Curcumin (supplements) | Antiplatelet; inhibits clotting cascade | 7 days |
| Vitamin E (supplements >400 IU) | Antiplatelet; antagonizes vitamin K | 7 days |
| Fish oil (supplements >3g) | Antiplatelet; reduced thromboxane A2 | 7-14 days |
| Dong quai | Coumarin content; anticoagulant | 14 days |
| Feverfew | Antiplatelet via PAF inhibition | 7 days |
| Willow bark | Salicylate content; antiplatelet | 14 days |
| Dan Shen (Salvia) | Potent anticoagulant | 14 days |
| Echinacea | Immune stimulant; may impair wound healing; allergic risk | 7 days |
| Astragalus | Immune stimulant; theoretical interference with wound healing | 7 days |
| Ephedra / Ma huang | Cardiovascular instability; hypertension; arrhythmias | 14 days |
| St. John's Wort | CYP induction alters anesthesia drug metabolism; serotonin syndrome risk with meperidine | 14 days (ideally 5 weeks due to enzyme induction half-life) |
| Kava | CNS depression interacts with anesthesia; hepatotoxicity | 14 days |
| Valerian | CNS depression; tolerance may require higher anesthesia doses | 7 days (taper; do not stop abruptly due to withdrawal) |
| Licorice | Fluid retention; electrolyte imbalance; interacts with antihypertensives | 7 days |

### Herbs That May Support Post-Surgical Recovery (After Clearance)

| Herb | Benefit | Timing |
|------|---------|--------|
| Arnica (topical) | Reduces bruising and swelling | Apply after wound closure; not on open wounds |
| Bromelain (pineapple enzyme) | Reduces post-operative edema and inflammation | Start 24-48 hours post-op; may increase bleeding if started too early |
| Turmeric (dietary amounts) | Anti-inflammatory; wound healing support | Safe in food amounts post-op |
| Aloe vera (topical) | Wound healing; burn recovery | Only on healed/closed wounds; not on surgical incisions |
| Calendula (topical) | Wound healing; anti-inflammatory | After wound closure |
| Gotu kola | Supports collagen synthesis and wound healing | Start 3-5 days post-op once bleeding risk is minimal |

### General Guidelines

- Provide patients with a comprehensive list of supplements to stop pre-operatively at the surgical pre-assessment
- Include ALL over-the-counter supplements, vitamins, and herbal teas in the review
- Restart herbs only after surgical clearance and hemostasis is confirmed
- Taper valerian and other sedative herbs rather than stopping abruptly to avoid withdrawal
- Inform anesthesiologist of all herb use, even if discontinued, as metabolic effects may persist
- NSAID herbs (turmeric, ginger, boswellia) may be beneficial post-op but should not replace prescribed anticoagulant prophylaxis
